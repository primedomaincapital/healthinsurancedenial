The Algorithm Said No: How AI Became the New Denial Machine

An 88-year-old woman breaks her hip, has surgery, and spends three weeks in a rehabilitation facility learning to walk again. Her doctor says she needs two more weeks. The insurer's computer says she has had enough. The denial arrives before the doctor's note does.

4 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The most consequential health insurance decision in America is increasingly made by software. A 2024 report by the U.S. Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, reviewing more than 280,000 pages of internal documents, found that UnitedHealth's post-acute care denial rate more than doubled after it began deploying predictive tools, climbing from 8.7 percent to 22.7 percent between 2019 and 2022. The tool at the center of it, nH Predict, was built by UnitedHealth's NaviHealth subsidiary, acquired by its Optum division in 2020 for more than $1 billion, and designed to predict how long Medicare Advantage patients in skilled nursing and rehabilitation facilities would need care.

The families of deceased Medicare Advantage members sued, alleging the tool overrode physician determinations with a 90 percent error rate — calculated from the share of payment denials reversed through internal appeals or administrative law judge rulings. In February 2025, U.S. District Judge John R. Tunheim ruled the class action in Lokken v. UnitedHealth Group could proceed on breach of contract and good faith claims, and in March 2025 the court ordered UnitedHealth to hand over broad discovery, including all documents analyzing nH Predict and records related to its AI review board. UnitedHealth disputes that nH Predict makes coverage determinations, describing it as a care-support and guidance tool, and maintains that medical directors make final decisions.

The numbers behind the fight

The Senate report was not the last word. In June 2026, the Department of Health and Human Services' Office of Inspector General published two reports examining prior authorization requests from June 2024 across 19 Medicare Advantage organizations. They found the 19 plans collectively denied 12 percent of skilled nursing facility admission requests, and when patients appealed, the plans overturned 95 percent of those denials. The OIG's own language was blunt: the extremely high overturn rate indicates that some enrollees were initially denied medically necessary care, and raises concerns about denials that were not appealed.

A January 2026 Health Affairs study by Stanford researchers found that 82 percent of appealed Medicare Advantage prior authorization denials are ultimately overturned in the patient's favor. The American Medical Association's March 2025 survey found AI tools accused of producing denial rates up to 16 times higher than historical norms in some cases. And the scrutiny is widening: in July 2026, Senators Richard Blumenthal and Josh Hawley wrote to UnitedHealthcare, Humana, and CVS Health demanding records on how each insurer makes post-acute care coverage decisions, including any use of algorithms or AI, and an inventory of the predictive technologies each company uses.

The rules are catching up

The CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Final Rule, CMS-0057-F, took effect January 1, 2026, introducing the most sweeping federal prior authorization reforms in decades. In July 2026, the House Ways and Means Committee approved a bill to reform and closely monitor prior authorization in Medicare Advantage, the Improving Seniors' Timely Access to Care Act of 2026. Health plans, for their part, have voluntarily eliminated roughly 6.5 million prior authorizations across markets, including more than 15 percent in Medicare Advantage, according to the Better Medicare Alliance.

The industry's answer is that the technology is also the fix. UnitedHealth's newer platforms — OptumReal, launched in October 2025, and PreCheck Prior Authorization — compress prescription approval times from more than eight hours to under 30 seconds, achieve a 96 percent first-submission approval rate, cut denials for missing information by 68 percent, and reduce appeals by 88 percent compared with the manual baseline. The company credits its AI investment program with compressing its medical cost ratio to its lowest level in two years.

The counterargument: two different machines

That defense deserves a fair hearing, because it points at a real distinction. The nH Predict system named in the lawsuit is an older predictive model for post-acute care, built to forecast length of stay. The newer tools are workflow engines that check paperwork, match codes, and route clean requests through in seconds. A system that approves a straightforward prescription in 30 seconds and a system that cuts off a stroke patient's rehabilitation are not the same machine, even if both carry the label AI.

But the distinction cuts both ways. If the new tools are genuinely better, the old denials still happened, the Senate numbers still stand, and the 90 percent error-rate allegation still awaits its day in court. Efficiency at the front door does not answer what happened at the back door. And a 95 percent overturn rate on appeal is not a sign of a system working. It is a sign of a system that denies first and asks questions only when the patient fights back — and most patients never do. Only about a third of patients appeal their denials at all.

What the denial machine means for patients

The practical lesson is the oldest one in health insurance: appeal. The data says the appeal wins far more often than it loses, and the system counts on most people not trying. Get the denial in writing, get the doctor's note in the file, and use the plan's own process before going outside it. The second lesson is documentary: keep every letter, every reference number, every name. The Senate investigation ran on 280,000 pages of internal documents. A patient's file should be at least complete enough to survive the same scrutiny.

The third lesson is structural. Prior authorization is no longer a clerk with a checklist. It is a statistical model with a business case, and the business case for denying care is measurable in basis points on a medical cost ratio. Until the rules require the model to show its work — which the 2026 CMS rule begins to do — the patient is arguing with a black box, and the black box has a head start.
