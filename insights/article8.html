The Denial Gap Between Medicare Advantage and Traditional Medicare

Medicare Advantage plans denied 7.7 percent of prior authorization requests in 2024; traditional Medicare barely uses prior authorization at all. For post-acute care, the gap is a chasm: 65 percent of long-term care requests denied. The gap is not paperwork. It is access.

7 min read · Prime Domain Capital

Two 75-year-olds break a hip on the same day. Both need two weeks in a rehabilitation facility after surgery. One has traditional Medicare. The other has Medicare Advantage. The first patient's rehabilitation is approved as a matter of course — traditional Medicare barely uses prior authorization at all. The second patient's request goes to the plan for approval, and the plan denies it. The two patients have the same injury, the same doctor, and the same Medicare benefit. They do not have the same access to care. The difference is the plan, and the data on the difference is now precise enough to measure in percentage points.

Medicare Advantage now covers more than half of all Medicare beneficiaries — 55 percent in 2026, up from 19 percent in 2007, with enrollment passing 35 million in February 2026. The program's pitch is coordination, extras, and cost control. Its mechanism is prior authorization: the plan must approve the care before it is delivered. Traditional Medicare, by contrast, pays for care after it is delivered, and requires prior authorization for only a small set of services. The two programs are the same benefit, administered by different rules, and the rules produce different denial rates.

The headline numbers come from KFF's analysis of CMS data, published January 2026. In 2024, Medicare Advantage insurers made 52.8 million prior authorization determinations — 1.7 requests per enrollee on average — and fully or partially denied 4.1 million of them, a 7.7 percent denial rate, up from 6.4 percent in 2023 and similar to 7.4 percent in 2022. Traditional Medicare, in the same year, received about 2 prior authorization requests per 100 beneficiaries — a rate of about 0.02 per enrollee — and denied 22.9 percent of the few requests it received, fewer than 150,000 in total. The denial rate is higher in traditional Medicare. The denial volume is incomparably higher in Medicare Advantage, because prior authorization is the exception in one program and the routine in the other. A Medicare Advantage enrollee faces prior authorization 85 times as often as a traditional Medicare beneficiary.

Where the gap becomes a chasm

The overall 7.7 percent rate conceals the services where the gap is widest. In June 2026, the Department of Health and Human Services' Office of Inspector General published two reports examining prior authorization for post-acute care — the rehabilitation, skilled nursing, and long-term care that follows a hospital stay. The 19 Medicare Advantage organizations in the review collectively denied 65 percent of requests for long-term care hospital admission, 54 percent of requests for inpatient rehabilitation facility admission, and 12 percent of requests for skilled nursing facility admission. Those are the most expensive types of post-acute care: long-term care hospitals average $49,000 per stay, rehabilitation facilities $24,000, skilled nursing $16,000.

The variation across insurers is as wide as the variation across services. CVS Health rejected 80 percent of requests for long-term care hospital admission. Humana and UnitedHealth Group both denied more than 70 percent of long-term care requests. UnitedHealth rejected 66 percent of rehabilitation facility requests; Humana and CVS Health rejected more than half. The 16 smaller plans in the review rejected an average of 42 percent of long-term care requests — still high, but roughly half the rate of the three largest insurers. The patient's chance of approval depends on the plan, not only on the patient's condition.

The contractor layer adds another gap. The OIG found that naviHealth, UnitedHealth's contractor, processed half of all skilled nursing facility admission requests in the review and denied 14 percent of them — a higher rate than plans that processed requests internally (11 percent) or other contractors (9 percent). When patients appealed, the plans overturned 97 percent of the denials issued by naviHealth. Nursing home residents faced the steepest odds of all: the plans denied requests for skilled nursing care from nursing home residents 40 percent of the time, against 11 percent for all other enrollees.

The appeal, and what it reveals

The appeal data is the most damning part of the comparison, because it measures the denials against themselves. In Medicare Advantage overall, only 11.5 percent of denied prior authorization requests were appealed in 2024 — but 80.7 percent of the appeals were partly or fully overturned, and more than eight in ten appealed denials have been overturned in every year since 2019. For skilled nursing facility denials, 18 percent were appealed and 95 percent of those were overturned. For long-term care hospital denials, 36 percent were appealed and 36 percent were overturned. For rehabilitation facility denials, 31 percent were appealed and 43 percent were overturned. The OIG's language was blunt: the extremely high overturn rate indicates that some enrollees were initially denied medically necessary care, and raises concerns about denials that were not appealed.

The insurer-level overturn rates vary as widely as the denial rates. Among Medicare Advantage insurers, the share of appeals resulting in a favorable decision was lowest for Kaiser Foundation Health Plan at 51.0 percent and highest for Centene Corporation at 95.5 percent. The pattern is consistent: the plans that deny the most also overturn the most on appeal, which is what a system that denies first and asks questions later looks like in the data.

Traditional Medicare has no comparable appeal pattern to measure, because it has almost no prior authorization to appeal. The program's denials come after the care is delivered, as claims denials, and the appeal path runs through redetermination, reconsideration, an administrative law judge, and the Medicare Appeals Council. The volume is small. The structural difference is the point: traditional Medicare's control is retrospective, and it rarely prevents the care from happening. Medicare Advantage's control is prospective, and it prevents the care from happening unless the plan agrees.

Is the gap paperwork, or access?

The plans' defense is that prior authorization is utilization management, not denial of care — that it steers patients to the right setting, prevents unnecessary procedures, and holds down premiums. There is evidence for the first part: the plans do approve most requests, and the overall denial rate of 7.7 percent is low in absolute terms. The 2026 data under the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization Rule, which requires plans to publish their metrics, shows standard denial rates of about 12 percent in Medicare Advantage for 2025, with UnitedHealth Group highest at 17 percent and Elevance lowest at 5 percent — a spread that suggests real differences in how plans apply the same rules.

The evidence against the defense is the post-acute data. A 65 percent denial rate for long-term care hospital admission is not utilization management. It is a barrier, and the barrier falls hardest on the patients with the highest needs — the ones recovering from surgery, the ones in nursing homes, the ones whose rehabilitation determines whether they walk again. The OIG's 2019 finding still stands: in a sample of denied prior authorization requests, 13 percent met Medicare coverage rules and likely would have been approved under traditional Medicare. The Senate investigation that preceded it found UnitedHealth's post-acute denial rate more than doubled after it deployed predictive tools, from 8.7 percent to 22.7 percent between 2019 and 2022. The gap is not a paperwork difference. It is a difference in who gets care, and when.

The counter to that conclusion is that traditional Medicare is not free of the same problem in a different form. Its low prior authorization volume reflects a program that pays first and asks later, and the asking later — the audits, the clawbacks, the recovery audit contractors — creates its own burden on providers and patients. The difference is who bears the burden and when: in traditional Medicare, the provider bears it after the care; in Medicare Advantage, the patient bears it before the care. For a 75-year-old who has just broken a hip, the timing is the difference that matters.

What the gap means for the choice

The choice between Medicare Advantage and traditional Medicare is usually presented as a choice between extras and freedom — the dental and vision benefits against the right to see any doctor. The data says the choice is also, and more consequentially, a choice about prior authorization: 1.7 requests per enrollee per year against 0.02, a 7.7 percent denial rate against a program that barely denies in advance, and for the most expensive post-acute care, a 65 percent denial rate against a program that does not ask first. The patient who chooses Medicare Advantage should choose the plan, not only the premium: the denial rate varies from 4.2 percent at Elevance to 12.8 percent at UnitedHealth Group, and the overturn rate on appeal varies from 51 percent at Kaiser to 95.5 percent at Centene. The plan is the access. And the patient who is denied should appeal, because the appeal wins four times out of five — and the system, for all its machinery, still reverses itself when someone asks it to look again.
