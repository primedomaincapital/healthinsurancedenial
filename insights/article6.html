The Denial Letter, Decoded: What the Codes on Your Explanation of Benefits Mean

CO-50. PR-1. N115. Your explanation of benefits is written in code — and the code tells you whether the denial is appealable, whether you owe the money, or whether the provider has to absorb it.

7 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The letter arrives in the mail, or the explanation of benefits appears in the patient portal, and the first thing the patient sees is a code: CO-50. Or PR-1. Or CO-97, with N115 after it. The letter does not say, in plain language, what happened. It says it in code — and the code is the difference between a denial the patient can appeal, a bill the patient owes, and a charge the provider has to absorb. The codes are standardized, they are published, and almost nobody who receives them knows how to read them.

The system has three parts, and they arrive together on every adjusted claim line. The group code — two letters — assigns financial responsibility: who absorbs the amount. The Claim Adjustment Reason Code, or CARC — a number — states why the payment changed. The Remittance Advice Remark Code, or RARC — a code beginning with N, M, or MA — adds the detail needed to interpret or act on the change. The three parts are maintained by X12, the standards body, and published by the Washington Publishing Company. The codes reach the patient on the explanation of benefits and the provider on the electronic remittance advice, the 835 file. Read the group code to learn who bears the amount, the CARC to learn why, and the RARC to learn what to do next.

The group code is the most important part, because it decides whose money is at stake. CO means contractual obligation: the adjustment belongs to the provider under its contract with the payer, and the provider generally cannot bill the patient for it. PR means patient responsibility: the amount is the patient's to pay — deductible, coinsurance, copayment, or a service the plan leaves to the member — and it moves to patient billing. OA means other adjustment, used when no other group fits, most famously when another payer is primary and the claim must follow coordination-of-benefits rules. PI means payer-initiated reduction, where the payer reduced payment on its own initiative and the patient is not liable but the provider may be able to appeal. The same CARC under two different group codes means two different actions: CO-50 is a write-off the provider generally cannot bill to the patient. PR-1 is money the patient owes.

The codes that deny the most claims

CO-50 is the code patients fear most, and the one most worth appealing. It means the payer found the service not medically necessary. It is the code behind the prior authorization denials that dominate Medicare Advantage — and the code behind the 95 percent overturn rate on appeal for skilled nursing facility denials in the federal oversight data. A CO-50 is the payer's judgment, not a fact, and it is overturned more often than it is upheld when the patient fights it. The RARC that often travels with it, N115, adds that the decision was based on a Local Coverage Determination — the payer's own policy document — which is itself appealable.

CO-97 means the service is included in the payment for another service — bundling. The payer is saying the procedure was part of a larger one already paid for, and it will not pay twice. CO-97 is common in surgery and imaging, and it is frequently wrong: the bundled service was genuinely separate, or the modifier that would have unbundled it was missing from the claim. The fix is usually a corrected claim with the right modifier, not an appeal. CO-16 means the claim or service lacks information or has a submission error — the payer rarely specifies what is missing, which is why the code is the most frustrating in the set. CMS itself states that some CARCs are too generic to communicate the reason without at least one remark code, and requires contractors to send one. CO-16 is the obvious case: on its own it tells the patient only that something is missing.

CO-197 means precertification or authorization was absent — the prior authorization was never obtained, or was obtained for the wrong service. It is a front-end workflow gap, and appeals have a low success rate, because the authorization cannot be obtained retroactively in most plans. CO-18 means a duplicate claim or service, and it is often a clearinghouse routing artifact rather than a real duplicate. CO-109 means the claim is not covered by this payer — an eligibility gap, the patient's coverage does not match the payer billed. CO-45 means the charge exceeds the fee schedule — a contractual adjustment the provider absorbs. CO-29 means the time limit for filing has expired. CO-96 means a non-covered charge. CO-170 means payment is denied when performed and billed by a provider of this specialty. CO-B9 means the patient is enrolled in an HMO and the service was not authorized. CO-B15 means the service requires authorization by the primary care physician.

The patient-responsibility codes are the ones that become bills. PR-1 is the deductible amount. PR-2 is coinsurance. PR-3 is the copayment. These are not denials. They are the patient's share under the plan, and they are the amounts that can legitimately appear on a patient statement. The distinction matters because a patient who sees PR-1 on the explanation of benefits and calls it a denial is arguing with the wrong code — and a provider who bills a patient for a CO amount is billing for something the contract says the provider absorbs.

Which codes are appealable, and which are dead ends

The appealable codes are the judgment codes: CO-50 (medical necessity), CO-197 where the authorization was in fact obtained, CO-109 where the coverage is in fact in force, and CO-97 where the service was genuinely separate. These are decisions about the patient's care or coverage, and they can be reversed with evidence — the doctor's note, the authorization number, the coverage document. The dead ends are the structural codes: CO-29 (timely filing expired), CO-18 where the claim genuinely is a duplicate, and CO-45 (charge exceeds fee schedule), which is a contractual adjustment, not a decision about the patient. The codes that require a corrected claim rather than an appeal are CO-16 (missing information), CO-97 with a missing modifier, and CO-4 (the procedure code is inconsistent with the modifier used).

The top ten CARCs at most practices account for 70 to 80 percent of denial dollars, and most of those are addressable through front-end workflow fixes rather than back-end appeals. That is the provider's problem, not the patient's — but the patient who can read the code can tell the difference between a denial that needs a doctor's letter and a denial that needs a corrected claim, and can direct the provider's billing office to the right fix instead of waiting for the office to find it.

The counterargument: the code is not the care

There is a limit to what the code can tell the patient, and it is worth stating plainly. The code describes the payer's reason for the adjustment, not the medical reality of the care. A CO-50 does not mean the care was unnecessary. It means the payer's reviewer, applying the payer's criteria, concluded it was unnecessary — and the reviewer's criteria are the payer's policy, not the doctor's judgment. The 95 percent overturn rate on appealed skilled nursing denials is the measure of how often that judgment is wrong. The code is the payer's language, and the patient's job is to translate it, not to accept it.

There is also a limit to the appeal. Some denials are correct. Some services are not covered by the plan, some authorizations genuinely were not obtained, some claims genuinely are duplicates. The code does not tell the patient whether the denial is right. It tells the patient what the payer claims, and what the next step is. The patient who appeals every code wastes the time that the appealable ones need. The patient who appeals none of them pays for the payer's mistakes.

How to read the next letter

Find the group code first. If it is CO, the provider absorbs the amount and the patient should not be billed — and if a bill arrives anyway, the code is the patient's evidence. If it is PR, the amount is the patient's share, and the question is whether the deductible and coinsurance were calculated correctly. Then read the CARC: 50 is medical necessity, 97 is bundling, 16 is missing information, 197 is authorization, 18 is duplicate, 109 is coverage, 45 is fee schedule. Then read the RARC, if one is listed, for the detail — N115 means a Local Coverage Determination, N179 means additional information is needed. Then act on what the code says: appeal the judgment codes, correct the information codes, and pay the patient-responsibility codes after checking the math. The denial letter is not a verdict. It is a coded message, and the code is public. The patient who can read it is the patient who can answer it.
