How Much an Appeal Costs — and Who Pays When You Win

An external review costs the patient $25 at most — and nothing at all in most states. The real price of an appeal is measured in hours, medical records, and the size of the claim itself.

8 min read · Prime Domain Capital

The denial arrived for a $4,200 scan. The patient had two choices: pay it, or fight it. The fight would take phone calls, a letter from the doctor, a form, and a wait. The patient did the arithmetic that most patients never do — the cost of the appeal against the value of the claim — and the arithmetic said something surprising. The appeal was nearly free. The time was the price. And the time was worth less than the claim.

The appeal path has three stages, and each has a different price. The first is the internal appeal, also called a first-level appeal or reconsideration: the patient asks the insurer to review its own decision. It costs nothing in fees. The insurer must decide within 30 days for a pre-service request, or 60 days for a post-service claim, and within 72 hours for an expedited request where waiting would jeopardize the patient's health. The patient supplies the denial letter, the doctor's note, and the medical records. The insurer assigns the case to a reviewer who was not involved in the original decision. The cost is the patient's time — the phone calls, the records request, the letter — and the doctor's time, which the doctor usually donates because the doctor's office handles appeals as part of the practice.

The second stage is the external review, also called an independent review or, in some states, an external appeal. An independent review organization — a doctor or health care professional with no connection to the insurer, working in the same specialty as the care under review — examines the medical records and issues a final, binding decision. The federal rule caps the patient's cost at $25 per external review. In practice, most patients pay nothing: the federal HHS-administered process charges nothing, Oklahoma charges nothing, New Hampshire charges nothing, and the insurer pays the review organization's fees in every state. Massachusetts charges $25 per review, capped at $75 a year, waived for financial hardship, and refunded if the review is resolved completely in the patient's favor. New York charges $25 per appeal, capped at $75 a year, waived for Medicaid and for hardship, and refunded if the denial is overturned. The review organization itself is paid by the insurer — several hundred dollars per review — and the patient never sees that bill.

The third stage is the one most patients never reach: a lawyer, a state insurance department complaint, or a lawsuit. A lawyer for a health insurance appeal typically works on contingency for large claims or charges an hourly rate for small ones, and the economics rarely favor hiring counsel for a $4,200 scan. The state insurance department complaint is free and sometimes effective — the department can pressure the insurer, and the complaint creates a record. A lawsuit is the most expensive option and the least used, because the cost of litigation exceeds the value of most denied claims. The appeal path is designed to end at external review, and for most patients it does.

The time, priced honestly

The fees are small. The time is not, and the time is the real price of the appeal. The American Medical Association's surveys have documented the burden from the physician's side: 39 prior authorizations per physician per week, thirteen hours of paperwork. The patient's side is smaller but real: the denial letter must be read, the deadline noted — 180 days for most internal appeals, four months for external review in many states — the records gathered, the doctor's note requested, the form filed, the decision awaited. A standard external review takes 30 days in New York; an expedited review takes 72 hours, or 24 hours for a non-formulary drug. The internal appeal takes up to 30 days for a pre-service request. The full path, internal plus external, can take two to three months. The patient who needs the care now cannot wait two months, which is why the expedited track exists — and why the expedited track is the one to request whenever the care is urgent.

The records are the second cost. The patient needs the denial in writing, the doctor's note in the file, and the medical records that support the necessity. The doctor's office usually provides these without charge, but the patient must ask, and the asking takes a phone call and a follow-up. The external review agencies — IMEDECS, the Island Peer Review Organization (IPRO), MAXIMUS Federal Services — review the records the patient and the provider submit. A thin file loses. A complete file wins. The difference between the two is usually one phone call the patient did not make.

The odds, and who pays when the patient wins

The odds favor the patient who appeals, and they favor the patient by a wide margin. In Medicare Advantage, 80.7 percent of appealed prior authorization denials were partly or fully overturned in 2024, according to KFF's analysis of CMS data — and more than eight in ten appealed denials have been overturned in every year since 2019. For skilled nursing facility denials, the overturn rate on appeal was 95 percent in the federal oversight data from June 2026. In Massachusetts, over 40 percent of external review decisions are resolved in favor of the patient. The numbers say the same thing from every angle: the denial is often wrong, the appeal often fixes it, and the system counts on most patients not trying. Only 11.5 percent of Medicare Advantage denials were appealed in 2024.

When the patient wins, the insurer pays. The external review decision is binding on the plan: the service must be covered. The $25 fee is refunded in Massachusetts and New York when the denial is overturned. The insurer bears the cost of the review organization, the cost of the internal appeal, and the cost of the care. The patient bears the time. The arithmetic of the appeal is therefore simple: the fee is $25 or nothing, the time is a few hours spread over weeks, and the expected value — the claim multiplied by an 80 percent overturn rate — exceeds the cost whenever the claim is larger than a few hundred dollars.

There is one exception to the rule that the patient pays nothing: the attorney. A patient who hires a lawyer for a small claim will spend more on the lawyer than the claim is worth. The appeal path is designed for patients to walk alone, with the doctor's note and the records, and the external review is designed to be decided on the medicine rather than on the advocacy. The lawyer is for the claim large enough to justify the fee — the denied surgery, the denied hospitalization, the claim in the tens of thousands — and for the case where the insurer has denied repeatedly and the pattern itself is the evidence.

The counterargument: the appeal is the tax

There is a case against the appeal, and it is the case the insurers make implicitly every time they deny first and ask questions later. The appeal is a tax on the patient's time, and the tax falls hardest on the patients least able to pay it — the sick, the elderly, the ones without a doctor's office that handles appeals as a matter of routine. A system that denies 7.7 percent of prior authorization requests and overturns 80.7 percent of the denials that are appealed is a system that gets it wrong often and relies on the patient's effort to correct it. The 95 percent overturn rate for skilled nursing denials is not a sign of a system working. It is a sign of a system that denies first and asks questions only when the patient fights back — and most patients never do.

The counter to the counter is that the appeal works, and it works for the patients who use it. The external review is free, the overturn rate is high, and the decision is binding. The system's flaw is not that the appeal fails. It is that the appeal is necessary at all, and that the patients who need it most are the ones least likely to file it. The answer to that flaw is not to skip the appeal. It is to file it, and to tell the next patient to file it too.

Whether fighting is worth it

Judge the appeal by the claim, not by the effort. If the claim is larger than $500, the expected value of the appeal — the claim times an 80 percent overturn rate, minus $25 or nothing — exceeds the cost of the time. If the claim is a covered service the doctor says is necessary, the odds are better than 80 percent. If the denial is a CO-50, a medical-necessity denial, the appeal is the intended path and the overturn rate is the evidence. File the internal appeal within the deadline, request the external review if the internal appeal fails, and get the doctor's note in the file before either step. The appeal costs $25 at most, and usually nothing. The denial costs the full claim. The patient who does the arithmetic fights. The patient who does not pays.
